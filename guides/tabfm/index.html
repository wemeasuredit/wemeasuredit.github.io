A tutorial Tabular foundation models August 2026

Getting answers from a spreadsheet, without an expert and without much data

Most useful predictions in business are made from ordinary tables — customers in rows, facts about them in columns. Until recently, getting a computer to make those predictions well needed two things many organisations do not have: a specialist, and a great deal of data.

This article is about a new approach that needs neither. We will explain what it is, why it is an interesting idea in its own right, and then we will actually run it and show you every number we got.

What this article covers

We are going to take one realistic business question, try three different ways of answering it, and compare them honestly.

You do not need any background in computing or statistics. Every term is explained the first time it appears, and the important ones are explained again later, because this is a long article and nobody remembers everything.

Two of the three methods we will try are long-established and widely used. The third, called TabFM, was released by Google Research in June 2026 and works in a genuinely unusual way. Even if it turned out to perform poorly, it would be worth understanding, because the idea behind it is new and it changes what is possible for people without specialist skills. As it happens, it performed rather well, and we will show you exactly how well.

The question we are trying to answer

Imagine you work at a bank. You have a spreadsheet of customers: one row per customer, and columns holding facts about each one — their age, which country they live in, how much money is in their account, how long they have been a customer.

Now add one more column, which records whether that customer eventually closed their accounts and left. In business this is called churn, and it is expensive: winning a new customer costs far more than keeping an existing one.

For customers who left in the past, that column is filled in. For your current customers it is blank — and you would very much like to know what it will eventually say, because if you could see who was thinking of leaving, you could try to keep them.

So the task is simply this: fill in a missing column.

That is one of the most common prediction problems in business — far more common than anything involving images, speech or chatbots. Which customers will leave. Which invoices will not be paid. Which machines will fail. All of them are the same shape of problem: a table where one column is known for some rows and missing for others.

The data we will use, and why we made it ourselves

We are not using a real bank's data. We wrote a small program that invents 12,000 customers. That choice is important, so let us explain the reasoning before showing you the result.

With real data, nobody knows the true reasons people leave. So when a prediction method scores, say, seven out of ten, you have no way of telling whether that means "this method is mediocre" or "seven out of ten is the best anyone could ever do here". Those are completely different conclusions and you cannot distinguish them.

When we invent the data ourselves, we write the rule that decides who leaves. We therefore know the correct answer for every customer, and — more valuably — we can calculate the best score any method could possibly achieve. Every result in this article is measured against that known limit.

The rule we used to decide who leaves

We could have made this easy — for example, by declaring that everyone in Germany leaves and nobody else does. That would tell us nothing, because any method would find it immediately.

Instead we built a rule with the awkward properties that real human behaviour actually has. Each customer starts with a low baseline chance of leaving, and then their circumstances push that chance up or down.

Then we did two more things, and these are the ones that make the problem honest.

We added randomness that nothing can predict

Having worked out each customer's chance of leaving, we did not simply apply it. We first nudged it up or down by a random amount, and then flipped a weighted coin to decide the actual outcome.

This matters enormously. It means two customers with identical spreadsheet entries can have different fates — one leaves, one stays. And that is true of real life: people leave for reasons a spreadsheet cannot record. A customer moves abroad. A relative recommends another bank. Someone has a bad experience in a branch on a bad day. None of that is in your columns, and no amount of clever computing will recover it.

So perfect prediction is impossible here by construction, and that is deliberate. A test where perfection is achievable would not resemble any real problem.

We removed some of the data

We deleted the balance for about 5% of customers and the plan type for about 3%, chosen at random. Real spreadsheets always have gaps — a field was never filled in, a system failed, a record was migrated badly. A fair comparison has to include that inconvenience, because every method has to cope with it somehow.

Why this matters for everything that follows

Because we wrote the rule, we can calculate the best score achievable by any method — a method that somehow knew the exact rule we used, and whose only remaining handicap was the randomness nobody can predict. That number turns out to be 0.792 on the scale we are about to explain. Keep it in mind: it is the target every result in this article is measured against, and nothing can beat it.

Four words we will use throughout

Before comparing methods, we need a small vocabulary. There are only four terms, and none of them mean anything complicated.

A model is a formula with adjustable numbers inside it, which takes the facts about a customer and produces a prediction.

Training is the process of showing that formula a set of customers whose outcomes are already known, and adjusting its internal numbers until its predictions match what actually happened. The customers you show it are called the training examples, or sometimes just the training rows.

The thirteen columns describing each customer are the features. The column we are trying to predict — did they leave — is the target.

There is one more idea to hold on to, and it is the one that will matter most later: training permanently changes the model. Once you have trained it, you could throw away the examples entirely, because everything it learned now lives inside the formula. This sounds obvious. It is exactly what TabFM does not do.

How we will keep score

We need a fair way to measure how good a prediction is. The obvious way turns out to be badly misleading here, and understanding why is worth a few minutes, because the same trap catches a great many real projects.

Why "percentage correct" fails

The obvious measure is the percentage of customers a method gets right. Our test group contains 300 customers, of whom 82 left and 218 stayed. So 72.7% of them stayed.

Now consider a method that does no work at all — it simply predicts "this customer will stay" for everybody, without even looking at the data. It is right 72.7% of the time. It sounds respectable, and it is completely worthless: it never identifies a single customer at risk, which was the entire point of the exercise.

You might expect a genuinely excellent method to score far higher and expose the fraud. It does not — and this is the surprising part.

The do-nothing method

Percentage correct72.7%

Finds nobody. Saves nobody. Useless.

A method that knows the exact rule

Knows precisely how we generated the data, and predicts a customer will leave when their true risk is above 50%.

Percentage correct77.0%

The best knowledge possible, and only 4.3 points better. This is percentage correct; the same method scores 0.792 on the ranking measure below.

That gap is the whole problem. A method with perfect knowledge of the truth scores only about four points above a method that does nothing at all. Percentage correct simply cannot tell them apart, so it cannot tell you whether your work has any value.

There is a second reason it fails, and it is more practical. Of our 300 test customers, the method that knows the true rule flags only 43 as being more likely than not to leave. The highest true risk of any single customer in the group is 0.89 — nobody is certain to go. Because most people stay, being confident about any individual is rare, and a measure built on yes-or-no verdicts throws away nearly everything the prediction knows.

What you would actually do with these predictions

Think about how a retention team really works. They have 3,000 current customers and a team that can telephone perhaps 200 of them this month with a retention offer. Nobody is going to call all 3,000, and nobody needs a yes-or-no verdict on each one.

What they need is a ranking: put the customers most likely to leave at the top of the list, so that the 200 calls we can afford are spent on the 200 people most worth calling.

That is the real task, and it is why the yes-or-no measure is the wrong tool. We do not need to know whether a customer will leave. We need to know who is more likely to leave than whom.

The measure we will use instead

So we use a measure of ranking quality. Its usual name is ROC AUC, which is an unhelpful abbreviation for a genuinely simple idea. It asks one question:

Take one customer who left and one who stayed, both picked at random. How often does the method give the leaver the higher risk score?

If a method scores 0.500, it gets that right half the time, which is exactly what you would achieve by guessing. That is the score of the do-nothing method, and of a method that assigns risk at random. If it scores 1.000, it ranks every single leaver above every single stayer.

The two useful properties are these. It is unaffected by the fact that most customers stay, so it cannot be fooled the way percentage correct was. And it measures precisely the thing the retention team cares about: whether the right people are near the top of the list.

Two rulers, not two models

One thing is worth settling before any score appears, because it is the single easiest place to get lost. The two methods above have now been measured twice, and the two numbers you get are not on the same scale.

Reading down the two rows is the argument of this whole section. On the first ruler, the useless method and the best method possible are 4.3 points apart. On the second, they are at opposite ends of the scale.

The three numbers to remember

0.500 means no ability at all — the same as guessing.
0.792 is the best score achievable on this data by anything, because of the randomness we built in. It means putting a leaver above a stayer 79.2% of the time.
1.000 is perfect ranking, and is impossible here.

Every result from here on is one of these numbers. When you see 0.65, read it as "about halfway between useless and the best anyone could do".

The three methods we will compare

Two of these have been in everyday use for years. The third is the new one. All three will be given exactly the same customers to learn from, and tested on exactly the same 300 customers.

Established · the accurate one

Gradient boosting

Built out of decision trees. A decision tree is a flowchart of yes-or-no questions: "Has this customer used their account recently? If not, do they hold more than two bank products? If so, predict they will leave."

Gradient boosting builds hundreds of such flowcharts one after another, and each new one concentrates on the customers the previous ones got wrong. It is the standard choice for spreadsheet data and, given enough examples, it is very hard to beat.

Established · the simple one

Logistic regression

Gives every column a weight — a number saying how strongly that fact pushes a customer toward leaving — then adds the weights up and converts the total into a risk score.

Because it can only add things up, it struggles with our rule. It cannot express "two bank products is safer than either one product or four", because that is not a straight line. It does well early on and then stops improving.

New · released June 2026

TabFM

Never trains on your data at all. Its internal numbers are fixed and never change — they are identical before and after it sees your spreadsheet.

Instead, your examples are handed to it as reading material at the moment it makes each prediction. This is called in-context learning, and the next sections explain how it can possibly work.

The problem TabFM is trying to solve

Established methods need a great deal of data before they produce anything of any value at all. Not before they become excellent — before they become useful.

This is the practical barrier that stops most organisations from ever starting. You have a question worth answering, and perhaps forty examples you have labelled by hand. Forty is not enough, and everybody in the field will tell you so. To find out whether the idea has any merit, you must first spend months gathering thousands more examples — without knowing whether the answer at the end will be worth anything.

You will see this concretely in our results. With 25 training customers, gradient boosting scores exactly 0.500 — the guessing score. Not a poor result: no result. It examines 25 customers, finds it cannot justify a single split of the data, gives up, and predicts the same thing for everybody.

TabFM's claim is that it can give you a genuinely useful ranking from a few dozen examples. If that is true, it changes who can do this work and when. You could test an idea on a Friday afternoon with the data you already have, and only invest in gathering more if the early signs are promising.

How a model can learn from ten examples

This is the part worth slowing down for, because the natural explanation is wrong, and the true one is more interesting.

The natural guess is that TabFM has seen a spreadsheet like ours before — that somewhere in its preparation there was a bank customer table, with a country column containing France, Germany and Spain, and that it is recognising something familiar.

Every part of that guess is wrong.

Has it seen this table before?
No. These 12,000 customers were invented by our own program on the day of the test. They have never existed anywhere else, and no copy of them was ever published.
Did it learn from other banks' customer tables?
No — and this is the surprising part. Google states that TabFM was prepared entirely on invented data. Not mostly: entirely. It has never read a real spreadsheet of any kind, from any industry, at any point.
Does it know what France or Germany are?
No. It has no knowledge of countries, of banking, or of customers. Nobody has ever told it what a bank is.
Does it recognise our column headings?
No. Nothing it was prepared on had a column called "months idle" or "products held", because nothing it was prepared on described anything real. This is the one answer of the four that we could check for ourselves rather than take on trust, so we did. The test is the next thing on this page.

How we know it ignores the column headings

Three of those four answers rest on what Google says about how TabFM was prepared. The fourth — that it does not recognise our column headings — was something we could check for ourselves rather than take on trust, so we checked it.

The test is easy to describe. We gave TabFM our 300 test customers and wrote down the risk score it produced for each one: a number between 0 and 1 saying how likely that customer is to close their accounts. Then we handed it the very same 300 customers a second time, with the same values sitting in the same places, and changed nothing at all except the words written at the top of each column. If those words carry any meaning for the model, the scores have to move.

We tried three ways of changing them, each one harsher than the one before.

  • Headings stripped of meaning. Every heading was replaced by a label that says nothing: age became c1, years a customer became c2, and so on to c13.
  • Headings that are wrong. Rather than remove the meaning we made it false. Every name was shifted along one position, so the column holding each customer's balance was now labelled years a customer, the column holding their country was labelled months idle, and the column holding their age was labelled joined via. Every heading in the table was now wrong about the values underneath it.
  • Wrong headings, and disguised words as well. On top of the headings we also replaced the words inside the table itself: France became a1, Germany became a2, Basic became d1. We put the replacements in the same alphabetical order as the words they stood in for, so the only thing taken away was what the word means.

The scores did not move at all. Not "moved slightly", and not "moved by less than our margin of error". Under all three disguises, every one of the 300 customers came back with exactly the same score as before — identical to the last digit the computer is able to store.

Why we did not simply believe that

A result of "nothing changed whatsoever" is also exactly what you would see if the test itself were broken. Had we made a mistake and quietly handed the model the original, properly labelled table three times over, the outcome would have looked precisely like this. So before believing the result, we needed one more version of the test — one we already knew ought to come out differently.

In that version we left every heading alone and instead destroyed the contents of a single column. We took products held — how many separate bank products the customer has, a current account, a savings account, a credit card, a loan — which is one of the strongest signals of who leaves, and set it to the same value for every customer, removing all the information it carried.

That version did change the answer, and for very nearly everybody. 297 of the 300 customers came back with a different score, where disguising the headings had changed none of them. The largest single change was 0.21, meaning TabFM's view of how likely that person was to leave shifted by 21 percentage points. So the model was certainly reading the table we handed it. It simply was not reading the words at the top of the columns.

We then repeated the comparison with different amounts of training data — 10 training customers, 100, and 500 — and with a second random selection of which customers to train on. Every disguised run matched the properly labelled version exactly. We also ran the original table twice unchanged, to confirm that TabFM returns the same answer when nothing is altered. It does, which is what makes a difference of zero meaningful: it is genuinely no difference, rather than a difference too small for us to notice.

What this means when you come to use it

You cannot help TabFM by giving your columns clear, descriptive names, and you cannot mislead it by giving them wrong ones. It will not notice either way. Everything it concludes it concludes from the values themselves and the relationships between them — which is exactly what you would expect of a model whose entire preparation consisted of invented tables that described nothing real.

What it was actually shown

Google's team generated hundreds of millions of invented tables. Each one was produced by a recipe of the following kind: invent a few hidden causes; connect them to each other with randomly chosen mathematical relationships; add some randomness; then write down the result as a table of numbers with the correct answers attached.

The formal name for such a recipe is a structural causal model, but the plain description above is all you need. Run that recipe hundreds of millions of times with different random wiring each time, and you produce an enormous library of tiny invented worlds — each one a table in which something genuinely causes something else, and each one supplied with the right answers.

Google's stated reason for working this way is practical: genuinely varied real tables are far too scarce to prepare a model on at that scale, so inventing them was, in their words, the only workable option.

Why that produces the ability to learn from ten rows

Here is the key question. What is the only thing worth learning across millions of unrelated invented worlds?

Not the contents. The contents are different every time and describe nothing real. The only thing that carries over from one invented world to the next is the skill of working out the structure of a table from a small number of rows.

So what was learned is not knowledge about banks or customers. What was learned is the act of learning itself.

Gradient boosting also has a procedure for finding patterns, but it is a fixed one, written down by statisticians decades ago. TabFM's procedure was discovered, by being made to solve millions of small table-shaped puzzles until it became good at small table-shaped puzzles in general.

Now consider what happens when either method is given ten customers. The real difficulty with very little data is not that the answer is hidden — it is that too many different explanations fit the evidence equally well.

Gradient boosting has to commit to one. It must choose a flowchart it can statistically justify, and with ten customers it can justify nothing at all, so it declines to guess. TabFM does not have to commit. Having worked through millions of invented worlds, it carries expectations about how tables usually behave: that columns often interact, that effects often have thresholds, that a rare category can carry a strong signal, that some columns are pure noise, that values go missing. Faced with ten rows, it weighs the explanations that fit, leaning toward the kinds of structure that were common across all those worlds.

That accumulated expectation is what stands in for the data you do not have. It is the entire advantage — and it also explains why the advantage shrinks as examples accumulate. Once you have thousands of rows, the evidence speaks clearly for itself, and prior expectations stop being needed.

The same mechanism is also the risk

TabFM leans toward the kinds of patterns its invented worlds tended to contain. If your real problem is shaped quite unlike anything that recipe produces, that leaning is not help — it is a bias, and it can be confidently wrong. An expectation is worth exactly as much as it is appropriate. This is not a hypothetical concern, and it is the strongest reason to check a TabFM result against a traditional method once you have enough data to train one.

What TabFM actually is

We have described what TabFM learned. It is worth being equally clear about what it is, because that determines what it can and cannot do.

TabFM is a neural network: a large mathematical structure whose behaviour is controlled by a very large collection of numbers, called its weights. More specifically it is a transformer, which is the same broad design used by chatbots and language models. When you download TabFM you are downloading 6.56 gigabytes of these weights.

The mechanism that makes a transformer work is called attention. For each piece of the input, the network works out how much every other piece matters to it. In a chatbot, that is words attending to other words. In TabFM it operates across the table in both directions: rows attending to other rows, and columns attending to other columns. That is how it can notice both that one customer resembles another, and that one column only matters in combination with a second.

Two learning procedures, arrived at from opposite directions

There are two different things called algorithms in this story, and separating them makes everything clearer.

Gradient boosting and logistic regression are procedures written by people. They were designed, published, and printed in textbooks. You could read the steps, hand them to a programmer, and have them reimplemented. When you run one on your data, it produces a model.

TabFM works the other way around. What you download is not a procedure you can read; it is a collection of numbers. But when those numbers are run, what they carry out is a learning procedure — one that nobody designed, that has never been written down in any language, and that was instead discovered automatically while working through those millions of practice tables.

This is why TabFM is fairly described as a black box, and in a stronger sense than that phrase usually carries. It is worth separating the degrees, because "black box" gets used loosely:

  • Logistic regression is genuinely transparent. You can read the weight attached to each column and say out loud: "being inactive adds this much risk".
  • Gradient boosting is already difficult to interpret, because any single prediction is the combined verdict of hundreds of flowcharts.
  • TabFM is opaque at both levels. You cannot inspect its learning procedure, and you cannot easily explain why it judged one particular customer to be at risk.

One further consequence of being a neural network: a prediction is produced by a single pass through the network. There is no training loop, no repeated adjustment, nothing to wait for. That is why it needs no tuning, and also why it cannot carry anything over from one prediction to the next.

Two words you will meet everywhere else

Everything so far has been described in plain language, deliberately. But if you go on to read Google's own announcement, or almost anything else written about TabFM, you will meet two pieces of vocabulary in the first line. Both name ideas you have already understood, so it is worth attaching them to the right thing now rather than meeting them cold.

A foundation model is one large model that is prepared once, at considerable expense, and then used on many different problems without being rebuilt for any of them. That is the opposite of the usual arrangement, in which a fresh model is trained for every new question somebody wants answered. TabFM was prepared once by Google, and is then pointed at your table, and at everybody else's table, unchanged. The same word is used for the large models behind chatbots, for the same reason.

Zero-shot means the model gives an answer on a problem it was never specifically prepared for, in one go, with nothing about it adjusted first. "Shot" here means an attempt at adjusting the model to your particular problem, and zero of them are taken.

Google's announcement is titled "Introducing TabFM, a zero-shot foundation model for tabular data" — where tabular simply means arranged in a table, rows and columns, which is the only kind of data this model handles. Every part of that title now means something concrete: prepared once for everybody, used on your spreadsheet with no adjustment at all.

A reasonable question: if we download the weights, is it open source?

Not quite, and the distinction is worth getting right because it affects whether you can use it at work.

You genuinely do receive the weights. They are published openly, they sit on your disk, and you can inspect them. The usual term for this is open weights.

Open source normally means something more: that you may use the thing freely for any purpose, including commercially. For TabFM these two come apart. The program code that runs the model is released under a standard open source licence. The weights — the valuable part, the 6.56 gigabytes — are licensed for non-commercial use only. You may read them, run them, study them and publish research about them. You may not build a product you sell on top of them.

There is also a practical sense in which having the weights is not the same as having the answer. They are several billion numbers, and no individual number means anything a person could state. Being able to see them does not make the black box transparent.

Two kinds of question it can answer

Every question in this article has been the same shape: sort each customer into one of two named groups, left or stayed. Putting each row into one of a fixed set of named groups is called classification, and the groups themselves are called classes, or equivalently categories. Two classes is the simplest case, and it is the one we have been working with all along. Sorting support tickets into billing, technical and account would be three.

There is a second kind of question entirely, and TabFM answers it as well, although we did not test that side of it. Instead of a named group, what you want may be a quantity: how much this customer will spend next year, what this house will sell for, how many days until this machine needs servicing. Predicting a number rather than a group is called regression. Google states that TabFM handles both, in the same way and with the same single pass through the network.

That distinction matters more than it may sound, because the best known of TabFM's limits applies to only one of the two kinds of question.

TabFM comes with three published limits. They are not the same kind of constraint, which matters in practice: one of them will never change, and the other two move depending on your circumstances.

What we expected before we ran anything

Before running a single test, we wrote down what we thought TabFM would score at each training size, and committed to reporting the outcome against those guesses whether or not they held up. We are showing them for two reasons.

The first is honesty: it is easy to explain a result after the fact, and much harder to predict it beforehand. The second is more useful to you as a reader. Seeing a prediction sit beside a measurement makes it clear that these curves are specific results from one experiment, not laws of nature. Run the same comparison on different data, or with different settings, and the lines will fall differently.

How certain are these numbers?

Before drawing conclusions we should establish how much confidence any individual number deserves. This matters: without it, you cannot tell a real difference from an accident of which customers happened to be in the test.

Our test group contains 300 customers, of whom 82 left and 218 stayed. Had we chosen a different 300 customers, every score would have come out somewhat differently, purely by chance. Statisticians can calculate how much — for this kind of ranking score there is a standard formula, published by Hanley and McNeil in 1982, which takes the score, the number of leavers and the number of stayers, and returns the expected size of that wobble.

For our test group, a score around 0.78 carries an expected wobble of about 0.033. In everyday terms: if we repeated this whole exercise with a different 300 customers, a score of 0.78 might reasonably come out anywhere between about 0.72 and 0.84.

That is a wide range, and it is entirely a consequence of testing on only 300 people. Had we tested on 3,000 customers instead, the same calculation gives a wobble of about 0.010 — three times tighter. We used 300 because each TabFM result took several minutes to produce, and 3,000 would have taken roughly ten times longer.

How to read the results in light of that

Differences smaller than about 0.03 should be treated as ties. We cannot distinguish them. When one method scores 0.781 and another scores 0.773, the honest statement is that they performed similarly.

Differences larger than about 0.07 are real. When gradient boosting scores 0.500 and TabFM scores 0.614 on the same ten customers, that gap is far too large to be an accident of the test group.

A second kind of variation, and what it tells us

There is a separate source of wobble worth separating out. Each of our points is the average of three attempts with three different random selections of training customers — and those three results also differ from each other.

At 500 training customers, the three attempts scored 0.779, 0.784 and 0.780. At 1,000 they scored 0.778, 0.772 and 0.764. These are tight clusters, and that is informative: it tells us that which particular customers you train on barely matters, at least at these sizes.

But note carefully what it does not tell us. All three attempts were marked on the same 300 test customers, so their close agreement says nothing about the wobble caused by the test group. The two uncertainties are separate, and only the first one is reduced by averaging.

What the results show

With very few examples, only TabFM produces anything usable

Given ten training customers, gradient boosting scores 0.500 — no better than guessing, because it cannot justify a single division of the data. TabFM scores 0.614. That difference of 0.114 is well beyond the 0.03 wobble we calculated, so it is a real effect and not an accident of the test group.

In practical terms, 0.614 means that if you pick one customer who left and one who stayed, TabFM ranks them correctly about 61% of the time. That is not brilliant. But it is genuinely useful — a retention team working from that ranking would reach considerably more of the right people than one working from an alphabetical list, which is what a score of 0.500 amounts to.

TabFM reaches the maximum achievable score with 500 examples

At 500 training customers TabFM scores 0.781, against a maximum achievable score of 0.792. It has captured essentially everything the data has to offer.

Gradient boosting reaches 0.773 — a statistically indistinguishable result — but it needs 8,000 training customers to get there. That is sixteen times as much data for the same outcome.

For an organisation, that difference is not academic. Labelling 500 examples might take an afternoon. Labelling 8,000 might take a year, or be impossible.

On this data, TabFM was more accurate at every size we tested

We should state this plainly rather than imply it. From 10 training customers to 1,000, TabFM scored higher than both established methods at every single size we measured. Gradient boosting never overtook it within the range we tested.

Two honest qualifications belong immediately alongside that sentence. First, we stopped measuring TabFM at 1,000 customers, and gradient boosting was still improving at 8,000 — so we cannot say what would happen further out. Second, this is one dataset. A single experiment establishes that something can happen, not that it always will.

About the dip between 500 and 1,000

You may notice that TabFM's score goes slightly down between 500 customers (0.781) and 1,000 (0.772). It would be reasonable to wonder whether more data made it worse.

The difference is 0.009. Our calculated wobble is around 0.033, and the three individual attempts at 1,000 customers ranged from 0.764 to 0.778 all by themselves. The dip is comfortably smaller than the noise, so we cannot tell whether it is real, and we should not treat it as meaningful.

The sensible reading is that TabFM stopped improving somewhere around 500 customers — which is exactly what you would expect, because by then it had reached 0.781 against a hard maximum of 0.792. There was almost nothing left to gain.

Logistic regression behaves exactly as its design predicts

The simplest method starts respectably, at 0.587 with ten customers, and then levels off around 0.72 no matter how much data it receives. This is the expected consequence of a method that can only add up weighted columns: our invented rule contains curves, thresholds and interactions that no amount of adding up can express. More data cannot fix a limitation of form.

It is tempting to see something meaningful in that crossing point. There is nothing meaningful in it. It is a coincidence of this dataset, these three methods, and the particular settings we chose. On different data it would fall somewhere else, or not happen at all. Treat every curve in this article as a record of one experiment, not as a law.

What it costs to run

Accuracy is only half of a decision. The other half is what each method demands of you, and here the three differ enormously.

First, one setting that needs explaining

TabFM does not read your examples once. By default it reads them 32 times, varying slightly on each pass, and averages the answers. This technique is called an ensemble, and it is widely used: running something several times with small variations and averaging cancels out the quirks of any single run, giving a more stable result.

Each of those 32 passes costs time. For our tests we reduced this setting from 32 passes to one, because with 32 a single result would have taken about five hours.

It is important to understand which direction that adjustment cuts. Averaging 32 passes gives a better answer than taking one. So the real TabFM is somewhat more accurate than the version that produced our numbers. Every TabFM score in this article is therefore a conservative figure — the genuine article would do at least as well, probably slightly better.

Notice that TabFM's time grows with the number of examples — 80 seconds at 200, 213 seconds at 1,000 — while the established methods stay effectively instant. There is a reason for that, and it follows directly from how TabFM works.

Because its internal numbers never change, nothing it works out is ever retained. It re-reads your training examples from scratch for every single customer it assesses. Gradient boosting does its learning once, and from then on answers immediately, forever. That is the true price of not training.

What if you have no graphics card at all?

A graphics card is the usual assumption for running something like this, and for many people it is a real obstacle: they do not have one, and would rather not pay for one before knowing whether the idea is worth anything. So it is worth saying plainly that we ran TabFM without one.

On an ordinary computer processor, with no graphics card involved, learning from 100 examples and ranking all 300 test customers took 3 minutes and 16 seconds. The free graphics card took 80 seconds for a slightly harder version of the same job — learning from 200 examples rather than 100 — which makes the processor roughly three times slower. That is noticeably worse, and it is not the twenty-fold penalty you might reasonably have feared.

Two things are still required either way: the 6.56 gigabyte download, and enough memory to hold the model while it runs. The machine we used had 12.7 gigabytes of memory, and we would not expect a small laptop to manage.

The column-headings experiment described earlier in this article was run entirely this way, without a graphics card, because none was available to us that day. For a modest table and a few hundred customers to rank, an ordinary processor is a perfectly workable way of finding out whether TabFM is worth pursuing at all.

What this means if you are considering paying for faster hardware

We measured TabFM's cost precisely enough to plan with. It works out at roughly 0.19 seconds for each pass over each customer being ranked. So a full-strength run — 32 passes over 3,000 customers — comes to about five hours for a single result.

A rented graphics card of the kind available commercially is perhaps three to five times faster than the free one we used. That would bring a full-strength result down to roughly one to one and a half hours. Our full experiment needed 21 separate results, so even then it would run for well over a day.

The practical conclusion is that paying for hardware does not make the full-strength experiment comfortable — it makes a middle setting comfortable. For example, 8 passes over 1,000 customers costs about 25 minutes per result on free hardware, and around 6 minutes on rented hardware. That is a reasonable working setup, and it is what we would choose for a serious study.

So which should you use?

Here is our practical reading, given everything above.

Choosing between them
Your situationUseWhy
You have a few dozen labelled examples TabFM The established methods will return a result no better than guessing. TabFM will give you a usable ranking.
Labelling more examples is slow, costly or impossible TabFM Reaching a given quality on far fewer examples is a direct saving of time and money.
You have no specialist and want an answer this week TabFM Nothing to tune, no choices to get wrong, no data preparation.
You already have thousands of labelled examples Gradient boosting By then it is as accurate as TabFM was on this data, and it answers in under a second on a laptop instead of minutes on a graphics card.
You need many predictions, quickly or repeatedly Gradient boosting TabFM re-reads all its examples for every prediction, so it cannot keep up with high volumes.
It is going into a product you sell Gradient boosting TabFM's weights are licensed for non-commercial use only. This is a hard stop, regardless of how well it performs.
You need to sort rows into more than ten categories Gradient boosting TabFM can distinguish at most ten, and this is fixed in its design. It applies only to questions asking for a named group; if you are predicting a quantity, the limit does not arise.

To say the important part directly: on this data, TabFM was more accurate than gradient boosting at every training size we tested. Accuracy was not the reason to prefer the established method. The reasons to prefer it are speed, running cost, licensing, and the ten-category limit — and those are serious reasons that will decide many real projects.

The most useful way to think about it is as a sequence rather than a choice. Use TabFM at the start, when you have a promising question, a small pile of labelled examples, and no appetite for a long project to discover whether the idea has merit at all. If the early results are encouraging, gather more examples. Then switch to gradient boosting, which by that point will be equally accurate, will run on any laptop in under a second, and carries no licensing restriction.

What we do not know

Everything above came from one experiment on one invented dataset. Here is what that experiment genuinely could not establish, and what each gap would take to close.

How TabFM performs at full strength

Every TabFM score here came from a version reading the examples once instead of 32 times. Since averaging 32 readings produces a better answer than one, the real model is more accurate than our figures suggest — but we do not know by how much. It could be a rounding difference, or it could be a further 0.02. Closing this gap needs a faster graphics card and roughly a day of running.

What happens beyond 1,000 examples

We stopped measuring TabFM at 1,000 training customers because each result took three and a half minutes. Gradient boosting was still improving at 8,000. It is entirely possible that gradient boosting eventually overtakes TabFM, and we cannot say otherwise from our data. In practice this may not matter much, because both were already close to the 0.792 maximum by then — but "may not matter" is not the same as "we checked".

Whether any of this transfers to other problems

This is the largest gap. We tested one invented churn problem with thirteen columns and two possible answers. We do not know how TabFM behaves on data with hundreds of columns, on problems with several possible categories, on questions asking for a quantity rather than a named group, on genuinely messy real-world records, or on any question whose underlying structure is unlike the invented worlds TabFM practised on. Recall that its advantage comes precisely from expectations formed on those worlds — so a problem shaped unlike them is exactly where it would be expected to disappoint.

What other people have measured, and what their measurement cannot tell you

We are not the only people to have tested TabFM, and a far broader test than ours already exists. Google reports having evaluated it on TabArena, a public collection of real datasets kept for comparing methods of this kind: 38 of them asking for a named group and 13 asking for a quantity, ranging in size from 700 rows to 150,000. They report that TabFM came out ahead of established methods which had been carefully tuned for each dataset individually.

TabArena ranks methods by an Elo rating, the arrangement used to rank chess players. Every pair of methods is compared on every dataset, whichever one wins more of those head-to-head contests rises, and the rating summarises the whole tournament as a single number.

That collection covers far more ground than our one invented dataset, and it uses real data, which ours is not. On the question of whether TabFM works in general, it is the stronger evidence, and we would not pretend otherwise.

It answers a different question from ours, though, and the difference is worth seeing, because it is the reason this article is worth having alongside that benchmark. An Elo rating tells you which method beat which. It cannot tell you how much of the available signal was captured, because on real data nobody knows the rule that produced the outcomes, so nobody knows what the best possible score would have been. We do know ours, because we wrote the rule — which is what lets us say that 0.781 against a maximum of 0.792 means essentially everything the data had to offer, and not merely "better than the other two".

There is a second difference, and it is the more practical one. TabArena's smallest dataset has 700 rows. The claim that matters most to a reader who does not have much data — that you can get something usable from ten examples, or fifty — sits below the range that benchmark covers at all. That range is exactly what we measured.

How well it explains itself

We measured only ranking quality. We did not investigate whether TabFM can indicate why it considers a particular customer at risk, which in many regulated industries is a requirement rather than a nicety. Given that its learning procedure was never written down by anyone, we would expect this to be difficult.

Whether it holds up on data that is not invented

Our dataset was built to be realistic — curves, thresholds, interactions, unpredictable randomness and missing values. But it was built by us, and we knew its structure while designing the test. A genuinely convincing result would repeat this on several real datasets from different industries, where nobody knows the answer in advance.

Conclusions

This has been a long article, so here is the whole of it brought back together.

The problem. Filling in a missing column of a spreadsheet — which customers will leave, which invoices will go unpaid — is the most common prediction task in business. The established methods for doing it are accurate but need a great deal of labelled data before they produce anything useful at all, and they usually need a specialist.

What TabFM does differently. It never trains on your data. Its internal numbers are fixed, and your examples are read afresh each time it makes a prediction. It was prepared entirely on hundreds of millions of invented tables, which means it has never seen a real spreadsheet and does not know what a bank is. It does not read your column headings either — we checked that directly, by disguising the headings and finding that every prediction came back unchanged. What it learned from those invented worlds was not knowledge but a procedure: how to work out the structure of a table from very few rows.

What we measured. On our invented churn problem, with a maximum achievable score of 0.792: from ten labelled customers TabFM scored 0.614 while gradient boosting scored 0.500, which is the same as guessing. From 500 labelled customers TabFM scored 0.781, essentially the maximum. Gradient boosting needed 8,000 customers to reach a comparable 0.773 — sixteen times the data. Across every size we tested, TabFM was the more accurate method.

What it costs. A 6.56 gigabyte download, and between 49 seconds and three and a half minutes per result on a graphics card — against effectively zero for the established methods on an ordinary laptop. A graphics card is not strictly required: without one the same work takes about three times as long, which for a small table is a perfectly reasonable way to begin. Its weights may not be used in a commercial product. When sorting rows into named groups it can distinguish at most ten of them, although that limit does not arise when it is predicting a quantity instead.

How much to trust these numbers. Our test group of 300 customers gives every score a margin of roughly 0.03, so differences smaller than that are ties. This is one experiment on one invented dataset, and the curves are a record of what happened here, not laws that will hold elsewhere. Google has published a much broader test, on 51 real datasets, which is stronger evidence that TabFM works in general — but it starts at 700 rows, so it says nothing about the very small numbers of examples this article is about, and it cannot say how close any method came to the best score achievable.

The practical takeaway. TabFM changes what you can do at the beginning of a project, when you have a good question and hardly any labelled data. You can now get a genuine, useful answer from a few dozen examples, without hiring a specialist, and decide on that basis whether the idea deserves real investment. Once you have thousands of examples, the established methods become just as accurate and are far cheaper to run — so the sensible path is to start with TabFM and move on when the data justifies it.
